The town of Brackwater sits where two slow rivers meet before running east to the sea. For most of its history the town drew water from shallow wells, and every dry summer the wells near the market square turned salty. In the spring of 1891 the council hired an engineer named Ada Ferrin to find a better supply. She spent three months walking the hills to the north with a level, a notebook and a borrowed mule, and she came back with a plan that the newspaper called either brilliant or ruinous, depending on the week.

Ferrin proposed a reservoir in the Calder valley, eleven miles upstream, held back by an earth dam with a clay core. From the reservoir a brick aqueduct would carry water by gravity alone, dropping one foot in every two thousand, to a set of settling ponds on the edge of town. No pumps would be needed except for the houses on Signal Hill, which stood higher than the ponds. The council worried about cost, so Ferrin broke the work into stages: the dam first, then the aqueduct as far as the old toll bridge, and finally the ponds and the pipes under the streets.

The dam took four years. Workers lived in a camp of timber huts beside the river, and the camp grew a bakery, a school and a small hospital. Floods in the second winter washed away half of the clay core, and Ferrin rebuilt it with a wider base and a spillway cut into the rock on the eastern side. When the reservoir finally filled, it covered two farms and a chapel, whose bell tower still shows above the water in very dry years.

The aqueduct was harder to explain to the public than to build. People expected water to need a push, and many refused to believe that a slope too gentle to see could move it eleven miles. On the day the valves opened, a crowd gathered at the settling ponds and waited for most of an afternoon. The first water arrived just after five o'clock, brown with dust from the new brickwork, and it ran clear by the next morning.

Within ten years the town had doubled in size. Breweries and a paper mill moved to Brackwater because the water was soft and reliable, and the salty wells were capped with iron plates that can still be found under the pavement near the market. Ferrin later designed water systems for six other towns, but she said that Brackwater was the one she was proudest of, because it was the first time anyone had trusted her with something that would outlast her.

Today the reservoir supplies about forty thousand people. The original aqueduct still carries part of the flow, although a steel main laid in 1962 carries the rest. Engineers who inspect the brick channel each autumn report that it leaks less than the modern pipe. A small museum in the old valve house keeps Ferrin's notebooks, her level and a photograph of the mule, whose name, according to the label, was Patience.
